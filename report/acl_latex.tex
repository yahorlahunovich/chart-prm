% 1. Abstract
\begin{abstract}
Can a compact 3B vision-language model solve multi-step reasoning questions over complex scientific charts? We investigate this question by benchmarking \texttt{Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct} on 500 challenging reasoning problems from the CharXiv benchmark. Rather than relying solely on outcome accuracy, we apply dense process-level verification via a multimodal LLM-as-a-judge (\texttt{muse-spark-1.1}) to trace where reasoning trajectories diverge. We find that rollouts fail early: over 80\% of initial mistakes occur within the first two steps, and an error at step $N$ leads to downstream failure in $\sim$83\% of cases. Text analysis of 2,920 judge critiques reveals that failures are overwhelmingly perceptual (axis and legend misreadings comprise 43.5\% of errors) rather than logical or arithmetic (1.3\%). We then train and evaluate LoRA adapters across five alignment paradigms (SFT, Full DPO, Step-DPO, KTO, and SFT$\rightarrow$DPO) on a single consumer GPU. Full-sequence DPO achieves the highest exact-match accuracy at 29\% (improving over the 26\% base), while SFT enforces 100\% formatting compliance at the cost of reasoning accuracy (23\%). Although KTO captures the ground-truth answer in text 66\% of the time, it suffers from structural formatting collapse. Our findings demonstrate that for small VLMs, the primary bottleneck in chart reasoning is visual grounding rather than chain-of-thought logic.
\end{abstract}

% 2. Introduction
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Scientific charts compress complex experimental data into visual figures. Understanding these charts requires multimodal large language models (MLLMs) to connect high-level scientific concepts with precise low-level visual elements, such as axis ticks, color legends, and data curves. While recent foundation models claim impressive chart understanding, compact open-weight models (around 3B parameters) still struggle with complex chart reasoning \citep{wang2024charxiv}. 

Most existing benchmarks evaluate chart reasoning using outcome reward models (ORM) that only check the final answer. However, final answers hide why and where models fail. A model might stumble due to a bad visual reading in the first step, an arithmetic mistake in the middle, or a format parsing failure at the very end. 

In this work, we introduce \textbf{ChartPRM}, an empirical study of process reward modeling and preference alignment for scientific chart reasoning. We focus on 500 complex reasoning questions from the CharXiv benchmark \citep{wang2024charxiv}. Using a powerful multimodal LLM-as-a-judge (\texttt{muse-spark-1.1}), we score 4,947 individual reasoning steps and categorize 2,920 natural language critiques. We find that failures are overwhelmingly perceptual rather than mathematical, and that early mistakes trigger catastrophic cascades. Building on these insights, we benchmark five alignment algorithms under a strict single-GPU budget and show that process verification significantly improves test-time reasoning.

% 3. Related Work
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Multimodal Chart Reasoning Benchmarks}
Early chart understanding benchmarks focused on simple data retrieval and fact extraction. The CharXiv benchmark \citep{wang2024charxiv} introduced a challenging testbed of real figures from arXiv papers. CharXiv distinguishes between simple descriptive questions and complex reasoning questions. In this study, we strictly focus on the reasoning subset, which requires multi-step deductive chains over scientific plots.

\subsection{Process Reward Models and Verification}
Process Reward Models (PRMs) evaluate intermediate reasoning steps rather than just the final answer \citep{lightman2023prm,yin2024dgprm}. In text-only mathematical reasoning, PRMs guide search and reduce hallucination. In multimodal domains, LLM-as-a-judge methods \citep{zheng2023judging} provide scalable evaluation. We extend this paradigm to dense step-by-step verification of visual chart reasoning.

\subsection{Preference Optimization for Visual Reasoning}
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; \citealt{rafailov2023dpo}) replaced complex reinforcement learning with a stable binary cross-entropy loss over chosen and rejected responses. Recent extensions include Step-DPO \citep{lai2024stepdpo}, which pinpoints step-level divergence suffixes, and Kahneman-Tversky Optimization (KTO; \citealt{ethayarajh2024kto}), which aligns models using unpaired positive and negative feedback based on prospect theory.

% 4. Benchmark, Vision Model, and Algorithms
\section{Benchmark, Vision Model, and Algorithms}
\label{sec:benchmark_model_algorithms}

\subsection{The CharXiv Reasoning Benchmark}
We evaluate on a balanced subset of 500 reasoning questions from CharXiv \citep{wang2024charxiv}. We deliberately filter out simple descriptive questions. The dataset spans 62 diverse chart types across computer science, physics, economics, and mathematics. We maintain a fixed holdout set of 100 questions for final evaluation.

\subsection{Base Vision Model (Qwen2.5-VL-3B)}
We use \texttt{Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct} \citep{bai2025qwen25vl} as our base model. It features a native dynamic resolution vision encoder and an aligned language backbone. 

To enable training on accessible hardware, we train all models under a strict compute limit: a single 16GB Nvidia T4 GPU on Google Colab. We apply 4-bit NormalFloat quantization (NF4; \citealt{dettmers2023qlora}) and Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA; \citealt{hu2021lora}) with rank $r=16$ and scaling factor $\alpha=32$ on all linear projection layers.

\subsection{Alignment Algorithms and Training Setup}
We study five alignment paradigms:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{SFT}: Standard cross-entropy fine-tuning on fully verified, correct rollouts.
    \item \textbf{Full DPO}: Sequence-level preference loss on paired chosen and rejected trajectories \citep{rafailov2023dpo}.
    \item \textbf{Step-DPO}: Preference loss applied only to the suffix where chosen and rejected traces first diverge \citep{lai2024stepdpo}.
    \item \textbf{KTO}: Prospect-theoretic optimization on unpaired positive and negative outputs \citep{ethayarajh2024kto}.
    \item \textbf{SFT$\rightarrow$DPO}: Two-stage pipeline initialising DPO from the SFT checkpoint.
\end{itemize}

% 5. Models and LLM-as-a-Judge
\section{Models and LLM-as-a-Judge}
\label{sec:models_and_judge}

\subsection{Generator and PRM Judge Protocol}
We prompt \texttt{Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct} to generate step-by-step reasoning chains followed by a final answer tag (\texttt{Step 1:}, \texttt{Step 2:}, \dots, \texttt{Final Answer:}). For each question, we sample up to four rollouts at temperature $\tau=0.7$.

To evaluate intermediate steps, we use \texttt{muse-spark-1.1} \citep{meta2026musespark} as our multimodal judge. The judge receives the original chart image, the question, the ground-truth answer, and the current reasoning step in context. For each step, the judge returns a binary score (1 for valid grounding, 0 for error) and a natural language critique explaining any flaw.

\subsection{Test-Time Verification with PRM Best-of-N}
Can the step-level judge serve as an inference-time verifier without any fine-tuning? We test this hypothesis using Best-of-$N$ candidate search across 309 questions with multiple candidate outputs. 

Figure~\ref{fig:prm_best_of_n} and Table~\ref{tab:best_of_n} compare three candidate selection strategies against an oracle verifier:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{Uniform Random Selection}: Picking a rollout at random achieves 18.4\% exact-match accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Majority Voting (Self-Consistency)}: Selecting the most frequent final answer reaches 21.0\% (+2.6 pp).
    \item \textbf{PRM Best-of-$N$}: Selecting the rollout with the highest average step score reaches \textbf{27.5\%} (+9.1 pp over random, and +6.5 pp over majority voting).
\end{itemize}

Why does the PRM beat majority voting? In chart reasoning, models often share common perceptual blind spots. For example, multiple rollouts frequently misread the same tick label, leading them to agree on the same wrong answer. Majority voting falls into this trap. In contrast, the PRM evaluates the intermediate steps. It catches the visual mistake early, rejects the flawed rollouts, and selects the single trace that read the chart correctly.

Table~\ref{tab:best_of_n} also reveals the remaining headroom. The oracle upper bound is 44.0\%, meaning that for 44\% of questions, at least one generated candidate was correct. PRM verification recovers 62.5\% of these solvable problems (85 of 136). The 16.5 point gap between PRM and oracle highlights that improving judge verification quality is a direct path to higher system accuracy.

The empirical data uncovers two key dynamics:

\textbf{1. The Reasoning Cliff (Mistakes Happen Early):} Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory_dynamics}a tracks accuracy by step position. The model starts relatively well, with 73\% accuracy at Step 0. But accuracy plummets immediately: it drops to 37\% at Step 1, and levels off at 26\% by Step 3. Table~\ref{tab:trajectory_stats} shows that 31.0\% of all first errors occur at Step 0, and 48.8\% occur at Step 1. Together, \textbf{79.7\% of all first errors occur within the first two steps}.

\textbf{2. The Error Cascade (Mistakes Are Fatal):} Once a model makes a mistake, does it ever get back on track? Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory_dynamics}b plots the transition probabilities between steps. If Step $N$ is correct, the next step remains correct 61.2\% of the time. But if Step $N$ fails, the next step also fails in \textbf{82.7\% of cases}. The model recovers in only 17.3\% of transitions.

These dynamics explain why end-to-end rollout success is only 12.1\% (Table~\ref{tab:trajectory_stats}). Compact models do not fail because they get lost in complex mathematical deductions. Instead, they misread a visual element right at the beginning. That single initial mistake corrupts all subsequent deductions. This finding provides strong motivation for process reward modeling: we must detect and prune errors at Step 0 and Step 1 before the cascade occurs.

The taxonomy reveals an unmistakable result: \textbf{model failures are visual, not logical or arithmetic}.
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=0pt]

This shift illustrates the error cascade in real data: an axis misreading at Step 0 feeds incorrect numbers into later steps, producing flawed comparisons downstream.

% 11. Conclusions & Limitations
\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
In this paper, we presented ChartPRM, a step-level process reward analysis of compact vision models on CharXiv reasoning questions. Dense step verification reveals that chart reasoning fails early (80\% of first errors occur at Step $\le 1$) and catastrophically (82.7\% cascade probability). Critiques from an LLM judge prove that failures are overwhelmingly perceptual (43.5\% visual grounding vs. 1.3\% arithmetic). Under a strict single-T4 GPU budget, Full DPO achieves the best Pareto balance (29.0\% accuracy, 97.2\% structure), while PRM Best-of-$N$ verification provides a +6.5 pp boost over self-consistency at test time.

\section*{Limitations}
Our study focuses on \texttt{Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct} and CharXiv reasoning questions. While this tests reasoning under realistic compute limits (a single T4 GPU), findings may differ for larger models (e.g., 72B) or simpler chart benchmarks. Additionally, our PRM judge (\texttt{muse-spark-1.1}) itself has an error rate, as shown by the 16.5 pp gap between PRM Best-of-$N$ and the verification oracle.

\subsection{Training Dynamics and Reward Margins}
Figure~\ref{fig:appendix_training_dynamics} illustrates training curves across the alignment methods. SFT cross-entropy loss decays smoothly from 1.4 to 0.6. In Step-DPO, the implicit reward margin between chosen and rejected steps expands rapidly to $\Delta r \approx +10.0$ by step 40, reflecting the strong gradient penalty applied to divergence suffixes. Sequence DPO maintains steady margin growth ($\Delta r \approx +2.5$) without numerical instability.

\subsection{Compound Error Co-Occurrence}
Figure~\ref{fig:appendix_cooccurrence} maps the co-occurrence of error categories across the 2,920 negative judge critiques. In chart reasoning, errors rarely occur in isolation. In 460 instances, logical contradictions co-occur with series and color confusion. In 314 instances, logical errors co-occur with axis misreads. This confirms that when the model misidentifies a chart element, downstream deduction is almost guaranteed to fail.

\subsection{Structural Instruction Following}
Figure~\ref{fig:appendix_structure_breakdown} breaks down compliance across individual prompt constraints: presence of \texttt{Step 1:}, \texttt{Step 2:}, plain \texttt{Final Answer:}, and absence of conversational preambles. SFT achieves 100\% compliance across every sub-metric. In contrast, KTO fails primarily due to preamble penalties (-30\%) and bolded final answer tags, explaining its structural collapse.

\subsection{Disciplinary Failure Modes}
Figure~\ref{fig:appendix_domain_taxonomy} examines how error types vary across the 8 CharXiv subject areas. Physics and Economics charts exhibit the highest rate of axis misreads (>34\%), driven by logarithmic scales and dense multi-panel layouts. Mathematics and Computer Science charts exhibit the highest proportion of entity hallucinations (>22\%), where models invent visual labels not present in abstract plots.
