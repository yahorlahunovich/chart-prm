% 1. Abstract
\begin{abstract}
Can a compact 3B vision-language model reason over complex scientific charts, not just read numbers off them? We study \texttt{Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct} on two benchmarks: 500 reasoning problems from CharXiv and a matched pool from ChartQA. A multimodal LLM-as-a-judge (\texttt{muse-spark-1.1}) scores each reasoning step rather than only the final answer. Over 80\% of first errors happen within two steps, and once a step fails, the next fails 83\% of the time. Critique analysis of 2,920 failed steps points to perception, not arithmetic: axis and legend misreadings dominate. We train seven alignment methods on this signal (SFT, full DPO, Step-DPO, KTO, SimPO, Pareto-DPO, and SFT$\rightarrow$DPO) against the base model and ChartGemma. To keep this supervision from resting on one judge's scalar opinion, we cluster its critiques into a reward tree and select preference pairs by Pareto dominance across its criteria. That method wins on both benchmarks: Pareto-DPO reaches 30\% exact-match on CharXiv, ahead of a 26\% base, and 60\% on ChartQA after transfer, against a 43\% base, the largest gain of any method on either dataset. Chart reasoning in small models fails on perception rather than logic, and dominance-filtered process reward is what survives the move to a second dataset.
\end{abstract}

% 2. Introduction
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Reasoning over scientific charts requires multimodal models to interleave visual perception (resolving dual axes, tick scales, and legend mappings) with symbolic deduction (computing rates and comparing extrema). Standard Outcome Reward Models (ORMs) provide sparse feedback only on the final answer \citep{ouyang2022instructgpt}, failing to isolate whether an error stems from an early visual misreading or downstream arithmetic. In contrast, Process Reward Models (PRMs) provide dense, step-by-step verification to enable granular credit assignment \citep{lightman2023prm}.

In this paper, we investigate a central hypothesis: \textit{Can a compact vision-language model reason effectively over complex scientific charts when aligned with only a small, process-supervised dataset?} Rather than relying on tens of thousands of annotations, we study whether fewer than 150 verified contrastive preference pairs suffice to align a compact model under consumer-grade compute constraints. A second question follows directly from the first: once such process supervision is built for one chart-QA benchmark, does it stay specific to that benchmark, or does it transfer, unmodified, to a second one?

We evaluate this hypothesis on the \textbf{CharXiv} benchmark \citep{wang2024charxiv}, and later test transfer on \textbf{ChartQA} \citep{masry2022chartqa}. Discarding surface-level descriptive queries, we isolate a stratified subset of \textbf{500 multi-step reasoning questions} from CharXiv spanning 8 academic domains and 62 chart types. Operating within a single 16GB Nvidia T4 GPU budget, we benchmark \texttt{Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct} \citep{bai2025qwen25vl} using 4-bit NF4 quantization \citep{dettmers2023qlora} and LoRA \citep{hu2021lora}.

Using an LLM-as-a-judge (\texttt{muse-spark-1.1}) \citep{meta2026musespark, zheng2023judging} with rollout-level batching, we evaluate 1,274 rollouts (4,947 steps). Analyzing 2,920 refuted steps reveals two primary diagnostic findings: (1)~\textbf{Perceptual Bottleneck:} Failures are overwhelmingly visual: axis/layout misreads (24.0\%) and series/color confusion (19.5\%) account for 43.5\% of failed steps, whereas pure arithmetic errors represent only 1.3\% (1 in 77 errors); (2)~\textbf{Catastrophic Cascades:} 79.7\% of flawed traces break down in Steps 0--1, and once a step fails, the subsequent step fails 82.7\% of the time ($P(S_{N+1}=0 \mid S_N=0) = 82.7\%$).

Leveraging these diagnostics, we benchmark seven alignment strategies on a 100-question held-out set: SFT (70 traces), Full DPO (134 pairs) \citep{rafailov2023dpo}, Suffix Step-DPO (54 pairs) \citep{lai2024stepdpo}, KTO (84 pos / 252 neg) \citep{ethayarajh2024kto}, SFT$\rightarrow$DPO, the reference-free SimPO \citep{meng2024simpo}, and a Pareto-dominance-filtered variant of DPO introduced in this work. Confirming our small-data hypothesis, full-trajectory preference optimization is consistently the strongest paradigm: Full DPO reaches 29.0\% accuracy on just 134 pairs (+3.0 pp over the 26.0\% base), and Pareto-filtering the same pairs edges this further to 30.0\%, the best result on CharXiv. SFT enforces 100\% formatting compliance but lowers accuracy to 23.0\% via template rigidity, while KTO attains 66.0\% latent ground-truth recall in text but suffers structural format collapse.

A single binary judge score, however, is a coarse training signal: it cannot tell a rollout that misreads an axis apart from one that hallucinates a legend entry, even though both receive the same failing label. Motivated by Yin et al.'s Dynamic and Generalizable Process Reward Modeling (DG-PRM) \citep{yin2024dgprm}, we adapt this idea to chart reasoning: we distill the judge's failure critiques into a reward tree of 33 scored criteria grouped under the 9 error categories above, and instead of collapsing a rollout's scores to one scalar, we keep a 9-dimensional vector and accept a preference pair only when the correct rollout Pareto-dominates the incorrect one across every category at once. This is what produces the 30.0\% result. To test whether this dynamic judge is also generalizable, as DG-PRM's own name claims, we transfer the reward tree unmodified to ChartQA and retrain all seven methods there: the tree fires on ChartQA content at least as densely as on CharXiv, and Pareto-DPO wins by a wide margin, 60.0\% exact-match against a 43.3\% base, the largest gain of any method on either dataset. We additionally show the same judge is useful at inference time, without any training: reranking existing rollouts by step-level process reward reaches 27.5\% accuracy, ahead of majority-vote self-consistency (21.0\%) and random selection (18.4\%).

Our main contributions are: (1)~We validate the small-data alignment hypothesis on \texttt{Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct} under a single-T4 GPU budget using 134 PRM preference pairs; (2)~We uncover an empirical perceptual bottleneck (43.5\% visual grounding vs.\ 1.3\% arithmetic) and catastrophic cascades (82.7\% failure continuation) across 2,920 refuted steps; (3)~We benchmark seven alignment paradigms and chart the Pareto frontier between format compliance and latent reasoning recall; (4)~We adapt DG-PRM to chart reasoning, using Pareto-dominance filtering across 33 criteria to achieve 30.0\% exact-match on CharXiv; (5)~We demonstrate zero-retuning transfer to ChartQA, where Pareto-DPO achieves 60.0\% (+16.7 pp over base); and (6)~We show that test-time PRM candidate reranking reaches 27.5\% EM, surpassing majority-vote self-consistency.

% 4. The ChartPRM Framework (Core Implementation - 2 Pages)
\section{The ChartPRM Framework}
\label{sec:chartprm_framework}

The central goal of \textbf{ChartPRM} is to verify, diagnose, and align step-by-step visual reasoning on complex scientific charts. Scientific plots pack dense information into visual elements. Solving a reasoning question requires multiple distinct steps: locating the relevant panel, reading numerical axis scales, matching curve styles to legend labels, and performing logical comparisons.

ChartPRM decomposes this task into discrete reasoning steps, scores each step individually with a multimodal judge, and uses those step-level signals to guide preference optimization. Crucially, we also ask whether it is possible to align a compact 3B vision-language model using only a very small dataset of verified chart reasoning traces (fewer than 150 examples).

Below, we detail our reasoning protocol, rollout-batched multimodal judge, preference curation, alignment objectives, DynamicPRM tree construction, and test-time verification.

\subsection{Structured Step-by-Step Reasoning Protocol}
\label{subsec:reasoning_protocol}
To enable step-level verification, the vision-language model generates Chain-of-Thought reasoning traces \citep{wei2022cot} structured into sequential steps (\texttt{Step 1:}, \texttt{Step 2:}, \dots) ending with a concise prediction on its own line (\texttt{Final Answer: <answer>}) without conversational preambles (see Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_prompts} for the exact prompt template). Candidate rollouts are sampled at temperature $\tau = 0.7$ and top-$p = 0.9$ (up to four rollouts per question), yielding 1,274 complete rollouts across 500 CharXiv \citep{wang2024charxiv} reasoning problems. An automated parser extracts the prediction following \texttt{Final Answer:} and applies lowercase whitespace normalization to assess exact match against the ground truth.

\subsection{Multimodal Process Reward Modeling}
\label{subsec:prm_judge}
We evaluate intermediate reasoning steps using \texttt{muse-spark-1.1} \citep{meta2026musespark, zheng2023judging} as a multimodal LLM judge. To overcome token and upload limits, we introduce \textit{rollout-level batching}: we resize the chart image $I$ to $\le 512$\,px (retaining visual legibility while minimizing token usage) and evaluate all steps $(y_1, \dots, y_T)$ within a single request alongside the question $q$ and reference answer $y^*$. 

For each step $t$, the judge returns: (1) a \textbf{Binary Step Score} $s_t \in \{0, 1\}$, where $s_t = 1$ indicates correct visual grounding and deduction, while $s_t = 0$ penalizes axis misreads, color confusion, hallucinated elements, or calculation errors; and (2) a \textbf{Natural Language Critique} detailing the failure mode for failed steps. The overall process reward of trajectory $\tau$ is the mean step score:
\begin{equation}
    R(\tau) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T s_t
    \label{eq:process_reward}
\end{equation}
A trajectory is \textit{fully verified} only if every step passes ($R(\tau) = 1.0$).


\subsection{Preference Dataset Curation and Step Slicing}
\label{subsec:dataset_curation}
From 1,274 judged rollouts (4,947 steps), we curate datasets for distinct alignment paradigms:

\textbf{1. SFT Demonstrations:} We select rollouts with perfect process scores ($R(\tau) = 1.0$) and correct final answers, yielding 70 gold-standard reasoning traces of verified visual deduction.

\textbf{2. Sequence DPO Pairs:} For questions with multiple rollouts, we form pairs $(y_w, y_l)$ where $R(y_w) > R(y_l)$ or $y_w$ attains the correct final answer when $y_l$ does not, yielding 134 chosen-rejected pairs.

\textbf{3. Suffix Step-DPO Slicing:} Standard sequence DPO penalizes entire trajectories equally, ignoring early correct steps. To isolate credit assignment, Step-DPO identifies the first divergence point $t^* = \min \{ t \mid y_{w,t} \neq y_{l,t} \text{ or } s_t(y_l) = 0 \}$. Keeping shared prefix $y_{<t^*}$ as unpenalized prompt context, we optimize preference loss strictly over the divergent suffix, yielding 54 prefix-aligned pairs.

\textbf{4. KTO Unpaired Dataset:} Kahneman-Tversky Optimization uses unpaired completions. We partition traces into 84 desirable ($R \ge 0.75$ and correct answer) and 252 undesirable ($R < 0.5$ or wrong answer) rollouts (1:3 positive-to-negative ratio).

We align policy $\pi_\theta$ against unaligned base instruct model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ under a strict 16GB VRAM budget on Nvidia Tesla T4/P100 GPUs using 4-bit NF4 quantization \citep{dettmers2023qlora} and LoRA adapters ($r=16$) \citep{hu2021lora} on \texttt{Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct} \citep{bai2025qwen25vl}. Peak GPU memory remains under 14.2\,GB throughout training. Table~\ref{tab:training_setup} summarizes configurations, dataset sizes, learning rates, and wall-clock runtimes.

We investigate seven alignment paradigms (full objective formulas in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_objectives}): (1)~\textbf{SFT} maximizes gold step log-likelihood (Eq.~\ref{eq:sft_loss}); (2)~\textbf{Full-Sequence DPO} \citep{rafailov2023dpo} optimizes a Bradley-Terry preference model over entire trajectories:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta; \pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\mathbb{E} \Big[ \log \sigma \Big( & \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} \nonumber \\
    {} - & \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)} \Big) \Big]
    \label{eq:dpo_loss}
\end{align}
with $\beta = 0.1$; (3)~\textbf{Suffix Step-DPO} \citep{lai2024stepdpo} applies DPO loss solely to divergent suffixes from $t^*$, masking prefix tokens from gradients (Eq.~\ref{eq:stepdpo_loss}); (4)~\textbf{KTO} \citep{ethayarajh2024kto} optimizes unpaired utility over implicit rewards (Eq.~\ref{eq:kto_loss}); (5)~\textbf{Two-Stage SFT$\rightarrow$DPO} initializes policy and reference from the SFT checkpoint before DPO; (6)~\textbf{SimPO} \citep{meng2024simpo} replaces the reference model with length-normalized policy probabilities and a target margin $\gamma$ ($\beta = 2.0$, $\gamma/\beta = 0.5$, Eq.~\ref{eq:simpo_loss}), halving forward passes per step; and (7)~\textbf{Pareto-DPO} trains with the identical DPO loss but filters pairs via 9-dimensional Pareto dominance across judge criteria (\S\ref{subsec:dynamic_prm}).

\subsection{DynamicPRM}
\label{subsec:dynamic_prm}

A single binary score per step cannot distinguish an axis misreading from a hallucinated legend entry. Motivated by Dynamic and Generalizable Process Reward Modeling (DG-PRM) \citep{yin2024dgprm}, we replace the single scalar with a multi-criteria reward built from the judge's own failure language.

\textbf{Reward Tree Construction:} We reuse the 9 error categories from \S\ref{subsec:perceptual_bottleneck} as parent nodes (\textit{other/unspecified} excluded). Within each parent, we cluster failure critiques by MiniLM sentence embeddings ($k$-means), merge near-duplicates (cosine distance $\le 0.14$), and distill each surviving cluster into a rubric statement via one LLM call per parent. This yields \textbf{33 scored criteria} under 9 parents, requiring only 9 API calls for distillation.

\textbf{Dynamic Multi-Criteria Judge:} For each step, the judge inspects the 33-criterion tree and self-selects applicable criteria, scoring each on a 1--3 scale blind to ground-truth answers. Across 309 training-pool questions (1,199 rollouts, 4,652 steps), the judge selects 3.85 criteria per step on average with 0\% empty selections. While collapsing these scores to a single scalar does not beat the binary judge (-2.6 pp), the dynamic judge exposes a rich 9-dimensional failure profile per rollout.

\textbf{Pareto-Dominance Pair Selection:} Rather than reducing scores to a scalar mean, we retain the 9-dimensional vector (one score per parent category). A preference pair $(y_w, y_l)$ is retained only when $y_w$ Pareto-dominates $y_l$: at least as good on every category and strictly better on at least one. This discards pairs trading off across disparate failure axes (e.g., fewer hallucinations but worse axis reading). Across the 309-question pool, this yields \textbf{154 pairs}, trained with the identical DPO trainer and hyperparameters as Full DPO (Eq.~\ref{eq:dpo_loss}), achieving CharXiv's top result at \textbf{30.0\% exact-match} (Table~\ref{tab:main_benchmark}).

\textbf{Cross-Benchmark Generalization:} DG-PRM claims dynamic criteria are generalizable \citep{yin2024dgprm}. We test this directly in \S\ref{subsec:chartqa_generalization} by transferring the 33-criterion tree unmodified to ChartQA \citep{masry2022chartqa}, where all 33 criteria activate at high density (4.21 criteria/step) and Pareto-DPO wins decisively.

% 4. Results and Analysis
\section{Results and Analysis}
\label{sec:results_analysis}

We evaluate the empirical performance of multimodal chart reasoning across four dimensions: reasoning trajectory dynamics, failure mode taxonomy, preference alignment effectiveness, and cross-dataset generalization. First, we examine multi-step rollouts from the unaligned base model, tracking the accuracy cliff and error cascades across reasoning depth (\S\ref{subsec:trajectory_dynamics}). Second, we analyze 2,920 refuted reasoning steps to establish an empirical error taxonomy (\S\ref{subsec:perceptual_bottleneck}), demonstrating that failures stem overwhelmingly from visual perception rather than arithmetic. Third, we benchmark seven preference-aligned policy models on held-out scientific reasoning problems (\S\ref{subsec:alignment_benchmark}) and analyze the Pareto trade-off between reasoning accuracy and output structure compliance (\S\ref{subsec:accuracy_structure_tradeoff}). Fourth, we retrain and re-evaluate all seven methods on a second, disjoint benchmark, ChartQA, to test whether any of these findings are specific to CharXiv (\S\ref{subsec:chartqa_generalization}).

We evaluate 1,274 rollouts (4,947 steps) generated by \texttt{Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct} across 500 CharXiv reasoning problems. Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory_dynamics} tracks score progression and transition dynamics (full statistics in Table~\ref{tab:trajectory_stats}, Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_diagnostics}).

\textbf{The Reasoning Cliff:} Reasoning quality degrades rapidly with chain depth (Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory_dynamics}a). At Step 0 (initial inspection), the model achieves a 73.0\% pass rate, falling to 43.1\% at Step 1 and 26.3\% by Step 3. Overall, only 12.1\% of rollouts (154/1,274) complete all steps without error. Multi-step chart reasoning requires sequential grounding: isolating panels, reading axes, matching legends, and deducing trends. For a compact 3B model, maintaining visual fidelity across sequential steps proves difficult.

\textbf{Catastrophic Error Cascades (82.7\%):} When a step fails ($s_t = 0$), the immediate next step fails \textbf{82.7\% of the time} ($P(S_{N+1}=0 \mid S_N=0) = 82.7\%$), recovering to a valid step in only 17.3\% of cases (Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory_dynamics}b). Crucially, \textbf{79.7\%} of all flawed rollouts commit their first error within Steps 0--1 (31.0\% at Step 0, 48.8\% at Step 1).

\textbf{Why Process Verification Matters:} Errors do not accumulate gradually at the end of solutions; models stumble immediately. An initial misread serves as a poisoned premise: subsequent steps apply sound logic to corrupted coordinates or curves. This dynamic justifies process reward modeling \citep{lightman2023prm}. Outcome models (ORMs) observe only terminal failure, whereas PRMs pinpoint the divergence step, enabling verifiers to arrest cascades before they spread.

\subsection{The Perceptual Bottleneck: Why Chart Reasoning Fails}
\label{subsec:perceptual_bottleneck}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../charts/report_selected/judge_01_error_taxonomy.png}
  \caption{\textbf{Distribution of 2,920 Refuted Reasoning Steps.} Visual grounding errors (43.5\%) dwarf pure arithmetic errors (1.3\%).}
  \label{fig:taxonomy_dist}
\end{figure}

Analyzing the critiques across all 2,920 failed steps ($s_t = 0$), we categorize errors into a 9-class taxonomy (Table~\ref{tab:appendix_error_taxonomy}, Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_diagnostics}). Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy_dist} illustrates the distribution, while Figure~\ref{fig:error_by_step_depth} shows temporal shifts across depth.

\textbf{Perception vs.\ Arithmetic:} A central question is whether models fail due to flawed arithmetic or faulty perception. The taxonomy provides an unequivocal answer: failures are overwhelmingly perceptual. Axis and layout misreads account for 24.0\% ($N=702$) and series/legend confusion for 19.5\% ($N=568$), totaling \textbf{43.5\%} ($N=1,270$) visual grounding errors. In sharp contrast, pure arithmetic mistakes constitute only \textbf{1.3\%} ($N=38$), or 1 in every 77 errors. The model's computational reasoning is intact; its visual parsing is the bottleneck (e.g., confusing logarithmic for linear axes or swapping colored markers).

\textbf{Temporal Shift Across Steps:} As detailed in Figure~\ref{fig:error_by_step_depth}, error modes evolve along the trajectory. At Step 0, \textbf{78.5\%} of errors are axis or layout misreads. In Steps 2--4, errors shift toward comparative and logical failures: entity hallucinations (15.2\%), incorrect rankings/extrema (10.9\%), and logical contradictions (8.2\%). Downstream steps appear conceptually flawed, but are actually operating on corrupted visual inputs.

The 1.0 pp gap is not statistically significant on $N=100$, but both confirm that full-trajectory preference optimization provides the primary accuracy driver while preserving high structural compliance (97.0--97.2\%).

\textbf{The SFT Underperformance Paradox:} SFT on 70 verified traces achieves only \textbf{23.0\%} EM (-3.0 pp vs.\ base). Without negative feedback on subtle visual traps (e.g., distinguishing trend lines from bounds), SFT induces rigid template memorization rather than adaptable grounding. Reference-free SimPO matches the base model (26.0\%): its training tail preference accuracy reached only 51.5\%, showing that 134 pairs at one epoch provide insufficient signal without a reference model.

\textbf{Step-DPO, KTO, and SFT$\rightarrow$DPO:} Suffix Step-DPO achieves \textbf{25.0\%} EM, as prefix masking disrupts global output formatting. Unpaired KTO matches base EM at \textbf{26.0\%} (29.0\% token match) but suffers structural collapse, detailed below. Sequential SFT$\rightarrow$DPO attains only \textbf{22.0\%}, indicating that SFT warm-up introduces suboptimal inductive biases.

\textbf{Validating Small-Data Alignment:} Aligning a 3B VLM does not demand vast annotation corpora. Using 134 judge-curated preference pairs on a single T4 GPU, Full DPO delivers a +3.0 pp gain, and multi-criteria Pareto filtering boosts this to +4.0 pp, establishing state-of-the-art accuracy on CharXiv.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
We investigated process reward modeling and preference optimization for complex chart reasoning on compact 3B vision-language models under consumer GPU constraints. Diagnostic analysis of 2,920 refuted steps revealed an empirical perceptual bottleneck: 43.5\% of errors originate from visual grounding (axes, legend styles) rather than arithmetic (1.3\%), cascading with an 82.7\% failure continuation rate. On both CharXiv and ChartQA, Pareto-dominance-filtered DPO consistently achieves state-of-the-art reasoning accuracy (30.0\% and 60.0\% EM) using fewer than 155 contrastive pairs. Furthermore, the 33-criterion dynamic reward tree transfers across benchmarks without modification, validating the generalizability of structured process supervision.

\section*{Limitations}
Our study evaluates a 3B parameter model (\texttt{Qwen2.5-VL-3B}); scaling dynamics on 7B--72B architectures remain to be explored. Second, while CharXiv holdout evaluations use $N=100$ problems, ChartQA transfer is evaluated on $N=30$ human-authored questions due to API and compute constraints. Finally, rollout generation relies on teacher-free sampling; integrating Monte Carlo Tree Search at inference time presents an exciting avenue for future work.

\section{Alignment Objectives, Optimization Details, and Training Dynamics}
\label{sec:appendix_objectives}

In this section, we provide the full mathematical formulations, training hyperparameters, and empirical loss trajectories for the preference alignment paradigms investigated in \S\ref{subsec:alignment_objectives}.

\subsection{Mathematical Formulations}
\label{subsec:appendix_formulas}

\textbf{1. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):} Demonstrations are trained by maximizing the conditional log-likelihood of verified gold reasoning steps:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w)} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_w|} \log \pi_\theta(y_{w,t} \mid x, y_{w,<t})
    \label{eq:sft_loss}
\end{equation}

\textbf{2. Full-Sequence DPO:} Sequence-level Direct Preference Optimization \citep{rafailov2023dpo} is defined in the main text (Equation~\ref{eq:dpo_loss}) with Bradley-Terry parameter $\beta = 0.1$.

\textbf{3. Suffix Step-DPO:} Suffix Step-DPO \citep{lai2024stepdpo} isolates the credit assignment problem by masking the shared prefix $y_{<t^*}$ out of the gradient, applying the DPO loss strictly to the divergent suffix starting at the first point of divergence $t^*$:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{Step-DPO}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E} \Big[ \log \sigma \Big( & \beta \sum_{k \ge t^*} \log \tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{w,k})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w,k})} \nonumber \\
    {} - & \beta \sum_{k \ge t^*} \log \tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{l,k})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l,k})} \Big) \Big]
    \label{eq:stepdpo_loss}
\end{align}

\textbf{4. Kahneman-Tversky Optimization (KTO):} KTO \citep{ethayarajh2024kto} optimizes policy weights on unpaired desirable ($y \in Y_{\text{pos}}$) and undesirable ($y \in Y_{\text{neg}}$) reasoning traces via prospect-theoretic value functions:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{KTO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{y} \left[ w(y) \left( 1 - v_\beta \left( \hat{r}_\theta(x, y) - z_{\text{ref}} \right) \right) \right]
    \label{eq:kto_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$ is the implicit reward, $v_\beta(r) = \sigma(\beta r)$ for desirable rollouts, $v_\beta(r) = \sigma(-\beta r)$ for undesirable rollouts, $w(y)$ is an importance weight ensuring balanced gradients, and $z_{\text{ref}}$ is a running exponential moving average estimating the KL divergence baseline.

\textbf{5. Simple Preference Optimization (SimPO):} SimPO \citep{meng2024simpo} eliminates the reference policy forward pass by substituting reference log-probabilities with length-normalized policy log-probabilities and an explicit target margin $\gamma$:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SimPO}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E} \Big[ \log \sigma \Big( & \tfrac{\beta}{|y_w|} \log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x) \nonumber \\
    {} - & \tfrac{\beta}{|y_l|} \log \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x) - \gamma \Big) \Big]
    \label{eq:simpo_loss}
\end{align}
We set $\beta = 2.0$ and target margin $\gamma = 1.0$ ($\gamma/\beta = 0.5$).

\subsection{Optimization Details and Hardware Footprint}
\label{subsec:appendix_hardware}

All fine-tuning experiments were conducted under a strict 16GB VRAM hardware ceiling on a single Nvidia Tesla T4 GPU (Google Colab) or an Nvidia Tesla P100 GPU (Kaggle). To operate within this memory constraint:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=1pt]
    \item Models are initialized in 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) quantization using \texttt{bitsandbytes} \citep{dettmers2023qlora}.
    \item Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) \citep{hu2021lora} is applied to all linear projection layers (attention query, key, value, output, and MLP up/down/gate projections) with rank $r = 16$, $\alpha = 32$, and dropout $0.05$.
    \item Gradients are accumulated across micro-batches of size 1 to achieve effective batch sizes of 4 or 8. Peak memory never exceeds 14.2\,GB throughout training.
\end{itemize}

\section{Prompt Protocol and Output Parsing}
\label{sec:appendix_prompts}

\subsection{Structured Chain-of-Thought Prompt}
\label{subsec:appendix_prompt_template}

To facilitate reliable process reward scoring, we design a structured Chain-of-Thought template that enforces explicit step boundaries and separates visual inspection from numerical deduction:
\begin{quote}
\small\ttfamily\raggedright
Solve the chart reasoning question step by step. Structure your answer strictly as follows:\\[2pt]
Step 1: <visual observation>\\
Step 2: <intermediate reading or deduction>\\
...\\
Final Answer: <concise answer>
\end{quote}

We enforce three practical formatting requirements during rollout evaluation:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep,topsep=1pt]
    \item \textbf{Indexed Steps:} Every reasoning step must begin with an explicit numeric label (\texttt{Step 1:}, \texttt{Step 2:}, etc.) followed by a newline.
    \item \textbf{Isolated Final Answer:} The final answer must appear on its own terminal line prefixed with \texttt{Final Answer:}, containing solely the extracted value or category without markdown asterisks or surrounding quotation marks.
    \item \textbf{Preamble Suppression:} Generations must omit conversational filler (e.g., ``Certainly! Let me analyze this plot.''). Any non-compliant opening text is recorded as a preamble violation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Automated Answer Parsing and Normalization}
\label{subsec:appendix_parser}

Evaluation rollouts are parsed by searching for the regex pattern \texttt{r"Final Answer:\textbackslash s*(.+)"} from the end of the text. Extracted answer strings undergo standard lowercase whitespace normalization (removing trailing punctuation, dollar signs, percent signs, and converting multi-space sequences to single spaces). Exact-match accuracy evaluates whether the normalized prediction matches the ground-truth string.

\subsection{Transfer Methodology and Dataset Selection}
\label{subsec:appendix_chartqa_methodology}

To test whether the 33-criterion dynamic reward tree generalises beyond CharXiv, we evaluate zero-retuning transfer on ChartQA \citep{masry2022chartqa}. We isolate 150 human-authored reasoning questions from ChartQA's test split for fine-tuning and hold out 30 questions for evaluation. Human-authored questions test complex multi-step reasoning over real-world charts, unlike machine-generated questions that use synthetic templates.

\subsection{Tree Activation and Generalization Results}
\label{subsec:appendix_chartqa_results}

The 33-criterion tree transfers unmodified to ChartQA without altering rubrics or parent categories. Across 612 rollouts on ChartQA, the dynamic judge (\texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite}) selects an average of 4.21 criteria per step with 0\% empty selections, confirming rubric generalizability.

Figure~\ref{fig:chartqa_overall} presents the holdout comparison. Pareto-DPO achieves 60.0\% exact match, 73.3\% token match, and 100.0\% structural compliance, outperforming the base model (43.3\% EM) by +16.7 pp. In contrast, Full DPO reaches 40.0\% EM (73.3\% structure) and Step-DPO reaches 36.7\% EM (59.3\% structure). Pareto filtering eliminates rollouts with cross-criterion trade-offs, yielding a clean contrastive signal that transfers robustly.
